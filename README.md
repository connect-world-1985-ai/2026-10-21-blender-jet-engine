# Procedural turbofan

A conceptual large high-bypass turbofan with an augmentor, built entirely with Blender's Python API and rendered headless with Cycles. The model is generated by `scripts/build_engine.py`. Nothing in the scene was placed by hand.

Blender 5.2.2 LTS runs headless in this environment. `blender -b --python-expr` works, and Cycles renders on the CPU with OpenImageDenoise. There is no CUDA device here (Cycles prints `CUEW initialization failed` and continues on the CPU).

Units are meters. The engine axis is +X, aft. +Z is up. The fan tip radius is 1.35 m, so the fan is 2.70 m across. The nacelle runs from about x = -0.7 m at the spinner to x = 8.3 m at the nozzle exit.

## What each part is

The geometry follows the gas path of a two-spool augmented turbofan. It is not a copy of a specific production engine. Stage counts, radii, and blade chords are chosen so the modules sit in the right order and the annulus does what that module does in a real engine.

**Fan and splitter.** Twenty-two wide-chord blades move the bulk of the air. Most of that air goes down the bypass duct and never enters the core. The spinner (with a dark spiral so the rotation reads on the ground) fairings the hub. Aft of the fan, thirty-four outlet guide vanes take the swirl out of the bypass stream so the thrust is axial. A rub strip and a carbon-fiber containment belt sit over the fan tips. The belt is there so a released blade stays inside the nacelle.

**Low-pressure compressor (booster).** Four rotor-and-stator stages on the low-pressure spool, inboard of the fan flow. They add a little pressure to the core stream before it reaches the high-pressure compressor. Blade height is the local annulus height, which is still comparatively tall here.

**High-pressure compressor.** Nine stages on the high-pressure spool. Each stage is a rotor row followed by a stator row. Annulus height falls as the air is compressed. The first three stator rows have variable vanes: stems out to a unison ring, the way an engine closes those vanes at low speed to keep the front stages off stall.

**Combustor.** An annular chamber between the compressor delivery and the high-pressure turbine. Eighteen fuel swirlers sit on the dome. The liners carry rows of small cooling holes and a few larger dilution holes, instanced with geometry nodes. An igniter stands off the case. A fuel manifold circles the front of the combustor. The orange volume inside is an emissive stand-in for the primary zone, not a simulated flame.

**High-pressure turbine.** One nozzle row and two rotor stages, shrouded, on the high-pressure shaft. The nozzles turn and accelerate the gas onto the rotors. The rotors drive the high-pressure compressor. Shrouds and knife seals are the outer band that keeps the tip leakage down.

**Low-pressure turbine.** One inlet nozzle row and four rotor stages, also shrouded, on the low-pressure shaft that runs forward to the fan. The gas path opens up as the gas expands. A tail cone closes the hub.

A real turbine repeats a nozzle row in front of every rotor. This model has the inlet nozzle and then the rotors. The interstage vanes are not separate rows.

**Mixer, afterburner, nozzle.** The bypass and core streams meet at a 16-lobe forced mixer. Behind that is an augmentor duct: two annular flame-holder gutters, radial spokes, twelve spray bars, and cooling holes in the liner. Sixteen convergent petals close to a throat, sixteen divergent petals open to the exit, and eight actuators stand outside the divergent row. A second emissive volume sits in the augmentor when it is lit.

**Shafts and bearings.** Two concentric shafts. The low-pressure shaft runs from the fan to the low-pressure turbine. The high-pressure shaft is the larger tube around it, from the high-pressure compressor to the high-pressure turbine. Four bearing stations (fan, HP front, HP rear, LP rear) are represented as races and a ring of rollers.

**Nacelle.** An elliptical inlet lip, a three-bay cowl with six panels around the circumference, an open panel gap over a dark sleeve, and rivet rows along the panel edges. Rivets and bolts are geometry-node instances on curves. A carbon access door sits on the fan cowl. A swept pylon stands on top. A small fairing under the fan case stands in for the accessory gearbox. Flanges and bolt circles mark the fan, compressor, and augmentor joints. A bleed pipe leaves the compressor case.

**Cutaway.** The `Cutaway` scene drops a 90-degree wedge on the lower port side, from -135° to -45° where the angle is `atan2(y, z)`. Shells are revolved only over the kept 270 degrees and capped. The cap faces use flat section colors: red on the low-pressure spool, blue on the high-pressure spool, orange on the hot section, grey on cold casings, purple on the augmentor. Blade rows omit any blade whose hub disk overlaps the wedge. `scripts/toggle_cutaway.py` switches the active scene when a window is open. Both scenes share mesh datablocks.

## How it is built

`scripts/tf/airfoil.py` builds the blade sections. Thickness is the NACA 65-010 table (Abbott and von Doenhoff / NACA Report 824), scaled to the local thickness ratio, with a circular leading edge blended into the table. Camber is the NACA 6-series mean line for uniform loading (`a = 0.5`). Sections are cosine-spaced, scaled to the local chord, staggered, and lofted. Chord is limited so neighboring blades do not intersect. Fan blades also sweep aft and lean. A short stack of rounded rectangles under the fan hub stands in for the shank.

`scripts/tf/meshlib.py` revolves meridional profiles about the engine axis, spins the lobed mixer (the radius waves with angle), and lofts the blade rows. Partial revolutions are generated directly with end caps. An earlier approach, cutting a full solid with a Boolean, tangled the edge net on hollow shells.

Casings, ducts, liners, shafts, disks, and petals are closed profiles revolved into quad shells. Petals are overlapped partial revolutions. Spray bars, actuator rods, variable-vane stems, and bearing rollers are cylinders.

Rivets, flange bolts, and combustor and augmentor holes are curves resampled in geometry nodes, with a small mesh instanced on the points and aligned to the local radial direction. The prototype meshes live in the `Assets` collection and are hidden from the render.

The low-pressure spool (fan, booster, low-pressure turbine) and the high-pressure spool are parented to two empties. Over the 192-frame shot the low-pressure spool turns 3.5 revolutions and the high-pressure spool turns 11.2, which is the right direction of the speed ratio even though both rates are slowed down so the blades stay readable.

## Materials

All of these are procedural Principled BSDF trees in `scripts/tf/materials.py`, except the carbon weave, which is a generated 768² image.

| Material | Where | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brushed titanium | Fan and compressor blades | Grey metal, anisotropic, fine chordwise roughness stripes, UV tangent |
| Spinner spiral | Spinner | White coat with one dark helical band |
| Nacelle paint | Cowl, pylon, fairing | Off-white with noise, a coat, and edge wear toward a primer color |
| Aluminum | Inlet lip, fan case | Brushed metal |
| Carbon weave | Containment belt, access door | 2/2 twill albedo, roughness, and a tangent-space normal, mapped on the shell UVs |
| Temper color | Turbine blades, turbine case, tail cone | Nickel-superalloy base that runs from blue/purple at the HP nozzle through straw to grey at the back of the LP turbine |
| Ceramic TBC | Combustor liners | Pale ceramic with soot staining and chip noise |
| Afterburner metal | Augmentor duct, petals | Dark heat-stained metal with a little emission |
| Flame | Combustor and augmentor | Noisy emission mixed with transparency |
| Section colors | Cut faces | Flat red, blue, orange, grey, purple |

## Lighting and color

The world is Poly Haven `studio_small_09` (2k HDR), by Sergej Majboroda, CC0. The file and the license note are in `2026-10-21-blend/hdri/`. The environment vector is the incoming ray, flipped and yawed so a softbox sits behind the camera instead of beside the nozzle. Four rectangle area lights (key, fill, rim, inlet) add shape. The view transform is AgX, look "AgX - Medium High Contrast". Hero, fan, turbine, and rivet cameras use depth of field. The side view is orthographic.

Cycles, CPU, OpenImageDenoise, adaptive sampling. Stills are 1920×1080, 64 samples. The machine has 4 CPU cores.

## Regenerate

```bash
blender -b -P scripts/build_engine.py
blender -b 2026-10-21-blend/turbofan.blend -P scripts/render_shots.py -- final
blender -b 2026-10-21-blend/turbofan.blend -P scripts/render_shots.py -- both
ffmpeg -y -framerate 24 -i 2026-10-21-renders/frames/turntable/frame_%04d.png \
  -c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p -crf 18 -movflags +faststart \
  2026-10-21-renders/turntable.mp4
ffmpeg -y -framerate 24 -i 2026-10-21-renders/frames/flythrough/frame_%04d.png \
  -c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p -crf 18 -movflags +faststart \
  2026-10-21-renders/flythrough.mp4
```

`render_shots.py` accepts `preview` (960×540, 16 samples), `final`, `turntable`, `fly`, or `both`.

## Quality

This is a procedural technical model that reads as an engine in a studio, not a substitute for a hand-built asset.

What holds up: the module order and the job of each module, real cambered sections on the blades, a dual spool with the right speed ratio, a cutaway that shows the core, stage counts that fall in a plausible range, rivet rows, a carbon weave you can see on the access door, turbine temper color, and a studio HDRI with depth of field.

What a professional model would still add: dovetail roots and fillets instead of a lofted shank, a nozzle vane between every turbine rotor, film-cooling holes as actual openings rather than instanced pegs, machined petal hinges, secondary-air pipes, a pylon with fairings and systems, photographed wear instead of noise, and displacement-level surface detail. Blade counts come from a solidity target, not from a certified drawing. The flame is an emissive shader. At hero distance the rivets become a dotted line, which is about right for an 8 mm head on a 9 m engine, and they are round pins rather than flush countersunk rivets.

Blender was not the limit. The limit is how much mechanical detail a single procedural builder describes. The scripts will rebuild the whole scene in a few seconds, so a later pass can replace one module without retopologizing the rest.
